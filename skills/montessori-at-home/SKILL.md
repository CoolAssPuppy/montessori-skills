# Start Montessori at home

Help a family choose one manageable next step from the home and child in front of them.

## When to use

The family wants to begin, simplify, or restart Montessori at home. They have a real room, a real child, and limited time.

If they want a hazard-by-hazard layout, use `prepared-home-environment`. If they already named one task such as dressing or pouring, use `everyday-independence`.

## Inputs

Ask only for what the plan needs:

- Age band and setting: under 3, 3-6, 6-12, or adolescent at home
- One recent interest or repeated action the adult actually saw
- Access, language, mobility, or sensory needs the family already knows
- What tools and furniture already exist
- Time and energy the adults can give this week
- Any medical, allergy, or swallowing instructions already in force

Do not request a full developmental history. Do not invent an interest the family did not report.

## Procedure

1. Restate the observed interest in factual language. "Stood at the sink and poured from a cup" is usable. "Ready for practical life" is not.
2. Match the age band to the kind of work, without a deadline. Under 3: one real care or movement task with close supervision. 3-6: a complete everyday task the child can repeat. 6-12: a project or reason-seeking follow-up (Montessori, *To Educate the Human Potential*, ch. I-II). Adolescent: a shared household or community responsibility the family actually holds (AMI excerpts from *From Childhood to Adolescence*, pp. 65-75).
3. Choose **one** accessible change that uses something already in the home: a low hook, a pitcher the child can lift, a basket for shoes, a stool that is stable. Prefer secondhand or ordinary tools. Hapa Family's "5 Lessons Learned After 5 Years of Montessori Parenting" argues for existing tools and against perfectionist setups; still screen small parts, cords, and climbable furniture.
4. Name **one** purposeful activity that belongs to real life in that home: wiping a spill, preparing a snack the child can eat safely, watering a plant, packing a bag. The adult demonstrates the difficult movement slowly, invites an attempt, and stays available. The child may refuse.
5. Write one review question for 3-7 days later: what the child did, what help was needed, what got in the way.
6. Stop. Do not add a second activity, a shopping list, or a classroom schedule.

Montessori's 1912 method (ch. V-VII) treats independence as something taught with patience and with tools the child can handle. It does not require a school shelf at home. Chapter VII's sample timetable was never followed in full; do not copy it as a home clock.

If the family raises a missed milestone or a worry that will not wait, point them to their clinician and [CDC Act Early](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/families/index.html). "Follow the child" does not delay that conversation.

## Output

A short starting plan:

- Observed interest (family's words, factual)
- One accessible change, cost, and who prepares it
- One purposeful activity, materials already owned, supervision note
- Review question and a date the family chose
- Assumptions labeled if a detail was missing

## Adaptations

Offer spoken, signed, gesture, or AAC invitations. Lower or raise work surfaces; keep a clear turning radius; provide a seated option. Use the home language for the invitation. If money is tight, name free or already-owned options only.

## Safeguards

No branded shopping list. No choking-size food or objects for a child who mouths. No floor mattress described as an approved infant sleep product. No spoiled food left out as a lesson. Adult help stays available.
